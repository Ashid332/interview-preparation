# Common Mistakes in Engineering Interviews

This document outlines common mistakes candidates make during engineering interviews, categorized by the level they are interviewing for.

## Junior / Entry-Level
- **Jumping Straight to Code:** Starting to write code before fully understanding the problem or edge cases.
- **Silent Coding:** Failing to communicate thought processes. The interviewer cannot grade what they cannot hear.
- **Giving Up Easily:** Getting stuck and freezing instead of asking for hints or talking through potential brute-force solutions.
- **Not Knowing the Basics:** Struggling with basic syntax of their chosen language or core data structures.
- **Ignoring Space Complexity:** Focusing only on time complexity and forgetting that memory usage matters.

## Mid-Level
- **Over-engineering:** Proposing overly complex architectures or design patterns for simple problems.
- **Lack of Testing Focus:** Writing code without considering how it would be tested or ignoring edge cases.
- **Weak Behavioral Answers:** Giving generic answers to behavioral questions without concrete metrics or specific examples of personal impact (failing to use the STAR method).
- **Buzzword Dropping:** Mentioning technologies (like Kafka or Kubernetes) in system design without understanding how they actually work under the hood.

## Senior
- **Failing to Drive the Interview:** Expecting the interviewer to handhold them through system design. Seniors should proactively define APIs, data models, and identify bottlenecks.
- **Ignoring Non-Functional Requirements:** Designing a system that works but ignoring scalability, fault tolerance, security, or observability.
- **"We" instead of "I":** In behavioral interviews, heavily relying on what the team accomplished rather than specifying their individual technical leadership and contributions.
- **Dogmatism:** Being overly attached to a specific technology or paradigm (e.g., "We must use microservices") without being able to discuss trade-offs objectively.

## Staff / Principal (Staff+)
- **Missing the Business Context:** Designing technical solutions that don't align with business goals, constraints, or ROI.
- **Lack of Organizational Scope:** Failing to demonstrate how they influence multiple teams, mentor senior engineers, or drive engineering culture.
- **Inability to Handle Ambiguity:** Staff engineers are given poorly defined problems. Failing to extract requirements from vague prompts is a red flag.
- **Getting Bogged Down in Details:** In high-level architecture discussions, spending too much time on implementation details of a single component rather than the overall system integration and cross-team dependencies.
