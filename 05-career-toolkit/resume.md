# Resume Engineering

As a hiring manager, I typically have about 6 to 10 seconds to look at your resume before deciding whether to put it in the "yes/maybe" pile or the "no" pile. Your resume is a highly constrained interface, and its only job is to get you a phone screen.

## The Prime Directive: Impact over Implementation

Most engineers write their resumes like a Jira ticket: "Implemented feature X using React and Node.js." 

This tells me what you did, but not why it mattered. I want to see your impact. Use the **XYZ formula**: "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]."

*   **Bad:** "Refactored the database queries."
*   **Good:** "Reduced API response time by 40% (Y) by refactoring N+1 queries (Z), leading to a smoother user experience during peak load (X)."

## Structural Best Practices

1.  **Keep it to one page** unless you have 10+ years of highly relevant, diverse experience. Even then, try for one page. Dense, well-organized information is better than fluff.
2.  **No objective statements.** We know your objective is to get a job. Replace it with a "Summary" only if you are pivoting careers; otherwise, jump straight to Experience.
3.  **Experience first.** Put your work history at the top. Education goes at the bottom.
4.  **Skills matrix.** Group your skills cleanly (e.g., "Languages:", "Frameworks:", "Infrastructure:"). Don't rate yourself out of 5 stars; just list the technologies you are comfortable discussing in an interview.

## Red Flags I Look Out For
*   **Keyword Stuffing:** Listing 50 technologies you used once in a tutorial. If it's on your resume, it's fair game for me to ask you deep technical questions about it.
*   **Typos and Formatting Inconsistencies:** If you can't indent a bullet point correctly, I'm going to worry about your code formatting.
*   **Lack of Metrics:** If you can't quantify your work, it tells me you might not understand the business value of what you're building.
